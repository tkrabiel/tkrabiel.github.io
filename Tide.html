📘 The “constitution” of tides — harmonic constituents

Tides aren't just one wave; they're a symphony of many periodic motions driven by the Moon, Sun, and Earth's rotation. Scientists (and NOAA) break tides into harmonic constituents — each with a name, speed (frequency), amplitude, and phase. Below are the major players.

M₂
Principal lunar semidiurnal — the Moon's main twice‑daily bulge. Largest constituent in most oceans.
🌕 period 12.42 h
S₂
Principal solar semidiurnal — due to the Sun. Slightly smaller, causes spring/neap cycle.
☀️ period 12.00 h
K₁
Luni-solar diurnal — once‑daily, accounts for declination effects of Moon and Sun.
🌍 period 23.93 h
O₁
Principal lunar diurnal — once‑daily, from Moon's declination. Important in diurnal regions.
🌙 period 25.82 h
N₂
Larger lunar elliptic semidiurnal — due to Moon's elliptical orbit. Modulates M₂.
📐 period 12.66 h
P₁
Solar diurnal — once‑daily solar constituent, smaller sibling of K₁.
☀️ period 24.07 h

👉 NOAA uses 37+ constituents for official predictions. Here we focus on the big six.

🔍 How to read the “take‑apart” table

Amplitude (H): half the tidal range contributed by that constituent (meters).
Phase (g): the lag (in degrees) of the maximum behind the equilibrium tide at Greenwich. Together with speed, it sets when high tide occurs.
Speed (σ): angular speed in degrees per hour — directly linked to astronomical cycles.
💡 Notice that semidiurnal (M₂, S₂) have speeds near 30°/h, diurnal (K₁, O₁) near 15°/h. The combination creates the daily inequality.
